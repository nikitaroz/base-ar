# Next-best-view and handheld scan guidance

Scope is practical next-best-view (NBV) and coverage guidance that could run on an iPhone during a short outdoor scan of one wall, the ground in front of it, and nearby equipment. The app output is a user instruction, not a robot path. Sources were checked on 26 September 2026. Research prototypes and shipping Apple APIs are labeled as such.

## Classic next-best-view for RGB-D and LiDAR, and what is real-time on a phone

### Takeaway
Classic NBV picks a 6-DoF camera pose by scoring unseen volume, information gain, or surface frontiers. Those scores are real-time on a robot computer only when the volume is coarse or the candidate set is tiny. The only measured phone timing found is a 2013 research app that planned one view in about 900 ms on an iPhone 5 using a 13³ voxel grid. Full ray-cast information gain on a fine occupancy map is not something these papers show running on a modern phone, and it needs a probabilistic volume ARKit does not provide.

### Cited Findings
- Scott, Roth, and Rivest survey view planning for automated 3D reconstruction and inspection and treat view planning as still open: find a small set of sensor poses for a reconstruction or inspection goal. The pipeline they describe is plan views, move the sensor, scan, register, and integrate. — [Scott, Roth, and Rivest 2003](https://doi.org/10.1145/641865.641868)
- Connolly’s 1985 algorithms use a partial octree of the scene to choose the next range-image view. — [Connolly 1985](https://doi.org/10.1109/robot.1985.1087372)
- Delmerico et al. split volumetric scores into counting metrics and probabilistic metrics. They attribute unknown-voxel counting to Connolly (1985) and Banta et al. (2000). They attribute frontier voxels — voxels on the border of free and unknown space — to Yamauchi (1997), and say that idea is used heavily for exploring an unknown environment rather than reconstructing one object. Vasquez-Gomez et al. (2014) count “occplane” voxels on the border of free and occluded space. Kriegel et al. (2015) use occupancy entropy. — [Delmerico et al., Autonomous Robots 2017](https://rpg.ifi.uzh.ch/docs/AURO17_Delmerico.pdf)
- The same paper defines information gain by ray casting from each candidate pose into a probabilistic voxel map (OctoMap) and summing a per-voxel score along each ray. A ray stops at a surface or at the map limit. The next view is the candidate that maximizes that sum, minus a travel cost. Formulations they compare: Occlusion Aware (entropy weighted by visibility likelihood), Unobserved Voxel (only voxels not yet observed), Rear Side Voxel (count rays that hit the unknown side of an already observed surface), Rear Side Entropy (entropy behind known surfaces), and Proximity Count (higher weight on unobserved voxels close to observed surfaces). They also compare Vasquez-Gomez Area Factor and Kriegel Average Entropy. — [Delmerico et al. 2017](https://rpg.ifi.uzh.ch/docs/AURO17_Delmerico.pdf)
- Their simulation places an unknown object of about 0.5 m in an empty scene with no ground plane, inside a 1.0 m cube, and scores 48 candidate views on a cylinder plus a half-sphere. The map resolution is 1 cm. They report computation time per view as much less than 10 seconds, small enough that robot travel dominates, and say cost is essentially the same across formulations. They do not report a phone or a mobile SoC. The scene is assumed spatially bounded. — [Delmerico et al. 2017](https://rpg.ifi.uzh.ch/docs/AURO17_Delmerico.pdf)
- In that same simulation, Area Factor and Proximity Count are the best early-coverage scores (best on 4 of 11 models each). Average Entropy reduces map entropy best but that does not track surface completeness, because entropy falls only after the same voxel is seen many times. Most surface completion happened in fewer than 10 well-chosen views; after about 6–10 views, coverage is asymptotic. Random views were competitive on average but with much higher variance. — [Delmerico et al. 2017](https://rpg.ifi.uzh.ch/docs/AURO17_Delmerico.pdf)
- Pito’s NBV solution finds unscanned areas of the scanner’s viewing volume and a place to put the scanner so those areas are sampled. SEE’s related work describes Pito’s method as building a partial mesh and picking views that observe mesh boundaries until the boundaries close, which needs a fixed workspace and a known sensor model. — [Pito 1999](https://doi.org/10.1109/34.799908); [Border, Gammell, and Newman 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08617)
- Surface Edge Explorer (SEE) does not keep a voxel grid. It classifies measured points by local density: core if enough neighbors lie inside resolution \(r\), outliers otherwise, and frontier points where core and outlier points meet. The next view is aimed at a frontier point, along the local surface normal, then adjusted to see around corners and occlusions. Complexity scales with the number of measurements, not the volume of the scene. On a simulated 40 m model of the Radcliffe Camera, SEE observed 99% of the model at 0.05 m resolution versus 79% for the best volumetric baseline (Average Entropy) in the same number of views, and the authors say volumetric ray tracing of a high-resolution grid is what gets expensive on that large model. This is a simulation on a known mesh, not a phone. — [Border, Gammell, and Newman 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08617)
- Bircher et al. grow a random tree in known free space and score branches by how much unmapped space they would see. Only the first edge is flown; the tree is rebuilt. They report the planner running online on an aerial robot with limited onboard compute. The score is unmapped volume, which is exploration rather than surface quality. — [Bircher et al., ICRA 2016](https://doi.org/10.1109/icra.2016.7487281)
- Batinović et al. replace full ray casting with shadow casting so an aerial LiDAR explorer can recompute information gain quickly enough for an onboard computer. The paper is an aerial robot, not a phone. — [Batinović, Ivanović, and Petrović 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.09323)
- Langguth and Goesele built a research app for the iPhone 5. After six seed photos, incremental structure-from-motion builds a sparse point cloud. They voxelize space around those points with \(N = 13\) cells per axis (grown by 1.5× as the scene grows), mark voxels along camera-to-point rays as observed, and search a surrounding sphere for a view that sees unknown voxels while staying within 30° of existing views so features still match. Planning itself took about 900 ms. Integrating a new image took 2–20 s, and 25–30 images was where global optimization became unreasonable. — [Langguth and Goesele, Eurographics 2013](https://doi.org/10.2312/conf/eg2013/short/093-096)
- Massios and Fisher add a quality criterion on top of a visibility criterion so the next view improves range-data quality, not only which surfaces are seen. — [Massios and Fisher, BMVC 1998](https://doi.org/10.5244/c.12.78)
- PC-NBV (Zeng, Zhao, and Liu, 2020) is a learned planner that takes a partial point cloud instead of converting it to voxels first. OpenAlex records 64 citations and no open PDF. It is a robot reconstruction network, not a measured phone system. — [OpenAlex record for PC-NBV](https://api.openalex.org/works?filter=title.search:PC-NBV)
- Placed et al. (IEEE T-RO, 2023) survey active SLAM: planning a robot’s motion to make the map accurate and complete. That is the large recent survey adjacent to NBV. It is about robot control, not instructions on a phone screen. — [Placed et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1109/tro.2023.3248510)
- ArXiv items from 2026 that match “next-best-view” and reconstruction are still robot or lab planners: MAGICIAN (CVPR 2026) plans long-horizon coverage with imagined 3D Gaussians for agents with an action space; a SIGGRAPH 2026 Bayesian NBV paper picks views to cut uncertainty in an implicit surface for a downstream task; ATAP (September 2026) plans views to verify a grasp affordance. None of the abstracts describe a phone UI or ARKit meshes. — [MAGICIAN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22650); [Zhu, Sellán, and Terenin, SIGGRAPH 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05095); [Cui et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23504)

### Inferences
- On a phone, the expensive piece is ray casting many candidate poses through a fine occupancy grid. Delmerico’s “much less than 10 s” is a desktop-class simulation of a 1 m object at 1 cm. A 3 m × 3 m × 2 m box at 1 cm is on the order of 18 million voxels, far past that setup. The same box at 10 cm is about 18,000 voxels, which is the regime where a handful of rays could be affordable, but that is an extrapolation, not a measured result.
- Methods that match ARKit’s actual output — a triangle mesh with normals, optional per-face class, camera pose, and optional per-pixel depth confidence — are the surface-frontier family (Pito boundaries, SEE density frontiers, Massios quality), not OctoMap entropy. ARKit does not hand the app a probabilistic TSDF.
- Learned 2020–2026 NBV networks are the wrong prototype: they need a trained model and a robot action space, and the 2026 samples are still not user-instruction systems.

### Gaps
- No paper found here reports information-gain ray casting frame rates on an iPhone 12–17 class device, or on ARKit mesh anchors specifically.
- Delmerico does not name the CPU behind the “<< 10 s” figure.
- SEE does not publish a wall-clock time in the text extracted here, only that it is faster than the volumetric baselines, especially on the 40 m model.
- The exact Massios quality formula (how angle of incidence is weighted) was not retrieved; only the claim that a quality term is added to visibility.
- A stable PDF URL for PC-NBV was not retrieved.

## Methods designed for a person holding the phone

### Takeaway
Shipping Apple guidance tells the person to fix capture quality (speed, light, distance, framing). It does not compute a next hole to walk toward. The clearest research system that does point a person at the next pose is Langguth and Goesele 2013: a ghost image to align with, an arrow, and a distance readout, on an iPhone 5, for walking around an object. RoomPlan’s public algorithm is a set of tiny classifiers for bad scan conditions, plus on-screen structure overlays.

### Cited Findings
- **RoomCaptureView (shipping API).** Apple describes it as managing the scan from start to finish: a camera feed, real-time graphic overlays on physical structures to show scanning progress, and text instructions when the framework needs a particular movement or perspective. When the app decides the scan is complete, the view shows a small-scale model for the user to approve. An app can ignore that view and draw its own graphics from `RoomCaptureSession`. The sample that demonstrates the view must run on a physical device with a LiDAR scanner, iOS 16 or later. The framework’s own one-line description of RoomPlan is creating a 3D model of a room by guiding people to scan with the camera. — [RoomCaptureView](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/roomplan/roomcaptureview); [RoomPlan](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/roomplan); [sample article](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/roomplan/create-a-3d-model-of-an-interior-room-by-guiding-the-user-through-an-ar-experience)
- **RoomPlan scan-guidance models (Apple Machine Learning Research, 4 October 2022, describes the shipping feature).** RoomPlan’s layout and object detectors consume semantic point clouds. To keep that input usable, RoomPlan shows real-time text: “Turn up the light,” “Slow down,” or “Move farther away.” Light uses average image luminance plus ARKit feature points, so a black cabinet is not treated as a dark room. Speed and distance use 3D camera-pose velocity and 2D image velocity, which together encode rotation, translation, and distance to elements already detected. Each detector is a two-layer MLP with dozens of parameters. Apple reports 90% accuracy on those three detection tasks. All of this runs on device, on the Neural Engine. They say a five-minute scan is long enough for rooms up to 15 m × 15 m. The article does not describe choosing a viewpoint to fill a hole, painting a region, or an arrow to a next pose. — [Apple, “3D Parametric Room Representation with RoomPlan”](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/roomplan)
- **ObjectCaptureView and `ObjectCaptureSession.Feedback` (shipping, RealityKit).** `ObjectCaptureView` is “a view that guides a user through capturing images for object capture” and presents the session state. Documented feedback cases, which are the user-facing conditions: `objectTooClose` (camera too close to track), `objectTooFar` (too far to capture well), `movingTooFast` (capture may pause), `outOfFieldOfView` (bounding box not in frame, so auto-capture will not run), `objectNotDetected` (manual box instead), `environmentTooDark` (auto-capture stops until light improves), `environmentLowLight` (capture continues, quality may suffer), `overCapturing` (further shots are not used for on-device reconstruction if on-device limits were enabled). Apple’s photo guide says adjacent shots need about 70% overlap, and below 50% the reconstruction may fail; avoid thin, highly reflective, transparent, or textureless objects. The 2024 sample “Scanning objects using Object Capture” requires a LiDAR scanner, A14 or later, and iOS/iPadOS 18, on a physical device. The documentation retrieved does not say the view draws an arrow to a hole in a scene mesh. — [ObjectCaptureView](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/realitykit/objectcaptureview); [Feedback](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/realitykit/objectcapturesession/feedback); [Capturing photographs for RealityKit Object Capture](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/realitykit/capturing-photographs-for-realitykit-object-capture); [Scanning objects using Object Capture](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/realitykit/scanning-objects-using-object-capture)
- **ARCoachingOverlayView (shipping, different job).** Standardized onboarding for world tracking and plane goals. It tells the person to move the device, sometimes to change speed, and hides once the goal is met (a plane found, or tracking established). It also appears for relocalization. It is not a completeness planner for an existing mesh. — [ARCoachingOverlayView](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/arcoachingoverlayview)
- **Langguth and Goesele 2013 (research prototype, not a product).** What the user sees: a semitransparent preview of the planned photo, made by projecting feature patches from neighboring views, which the person aligns with the live camera; an arrow in the image plane toward the planned 3D position; and a normalized distance. The shutter fires automatically when the viewing direction is within 10° of the plan. The person can reject a view. Navigation to a view typically took 5–30 s, longer when the preview was vague and the person had to trust the arrow. Tracking sometimes failed, which the authors treat as evidence the planned view would not have matched anyway. They compare themselves to Autodesk 123D Catch, which they say uploaded images and returned a model later, so the person got no immediate feedback. Their own system is offline from the network after capture starts, but the bundle adjustment is the bottleneck, not the view score. An outdoor statue trial is included. — [Langguth and Goesele 2013](https://doi.org/10.2312/conf/eg2013/short/093-096)
- ARKit’s mesh, which a custom guider would read, is a set of `ARMeshAnchor`s. Each face can be classified as `none`, `ceiling`, `door`, `floor`, `seat`, `table`, `wall`, or `window`. Scene depth, when the `sceneDepth` frame semantic is enabled on a LiDAR device, includes a confidence map with levels `low`, `medium`, and `high`. Apple says natural light makes confidence worse on highly reflective surfaces and on surfaces that absorb light. — [ARMeshClassification](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/armeshclassification); [scene reconstruction](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/arworldtrackingconfiguration/scenereconstruction); [sceneDepth](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/arframe/scenedepth); [confidence map](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/ardepthdata/confidencemap); [reconstructed-scene article](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/visualizing-and-interacting-with-a-reconstructed-scene)

### Inferences
- The phrase “scan the red region” and gravity-aware step arrows are a reasonable UI on top of a hole detector, but they are not what RoomPlan or Object Capture document. RoomPlan’s overlays show recognized structure; its sentences are about light, speed, and standoff. Object Capture’s documented sentences are about distance, speed, framing, light, and taking too many similar shots.
- For a hackathon, copying RoomPlan’s three sentences is easy and useful (slow down, move back, more light) and does not solve “the meter is missing.” Copying Langguth’s arrow-plus-ghost is the actual next-view UI, but their ghost image depends on sparse feature patches and breaks when the next view is a textureless wall.
- A gravity-aware “step left” is not described in these sources. It would be an app choice: take the hole centroid, project the offset into the ground plane using ARKit’s world up or `floor` faces, and map that to a short verb. That is implementable from the mesh; it is not a published Apple behavior.

### Gaps
- Apple does not publish how `RoomCaptureView` decides which overlay to draw, or any next-pose score. The ML article covers only the three quality notifications.
- The Object Capture documentation retrieved does not describe the on-screen graphic (whether there is an orbit ring, a progress count, or an arrow). Only the feedback enum and the fact of a guiding view are documented.
- No reliable public source was found here for how Polycam, Canvas, Matterport, or 3D Scanner App choose a next location or color holes. Those products were not used as evidence.
- No sourced system was found that uses the words “gravity-aware arrows” or “scan the red region” as its method.

## How these methods declare that scanning is finished

### Takeaway
Robot NBV papers stop when a bounded scene has no remaining frontier or when the best candidate’s information gain falls under a threshold, and they measure success as surface-coverage percent against a known model. Shipping phone APIs do not expose a coverage percent. RoomPlan leaves “done” to the app, then shows a model to approve. Object Capture stops using extra on-device photos once a shot limit or redundant-view condition hits. None of them declare “enough” because every semantic surface in an outdoor equipment scene has been seen.

### Cited Findings
- Delmerico et al. state a termination example: stop when the highest expected information gain of any candidate falls below a user threshold. Their experiments did not use it; they always ran 20 views. Surface coverage is the percent of ground-truth surface points that have a reconstructed point within a distance threshold. They also sum voxel entropy inside a 1.28 m cube. They observe that surface completion becomes asymptotic and that most of the model appears in a small number of views, which is a diminishing-returns statement measured against ground truth the phone will not have. — [Delmerico et al. 2017](https://rpg.ifi.uzh.ch/docs/AURO17_Delmerico.pdf)
- SEE stops when the last frontier point has been observed and every point is either core or an outlier, at a chosen resolution and density. The authors write that this assumes the observable scene is finite, and that in the real world you meet that assumption by defining a scene boundary and discarding measurements outside it. — [Border, Gammell, and Newman 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08617)
- Bircher et al. describe repeating the receding-horizon step until exploration is complete, with branch quality equal to unmapped space that could be seen. They do not define a coverage percent of a surface of interest in the abstract. — [Bircher et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1109/icra.2016.7487281)
- Yamauchi frontiers, as used by Delmerico, are for exploring unknown environment, so “done” means no frontier between free and unknown remains inside the mapped bounds. That is a volume criterion, not “the wall and the meter are covered.” — [Delmerico et al. 2017](https://rpg.ifi.uzh.ch/docs/AURO17_Delmerico.pdf)
- Langguth and Goesele do not publish a coverage-percent stop. In practice their loop becomes unusable around 25–30 images because bundle adjustment slows down, and the user can reject unreachable views. Success is judged afterwards by comparing a multi-view stereo model to an expert capture, not by an online threshold. — [Langguth and Goesele 2013](https://doi.org/10.2312/conf/eg2013/short/093-096)
- RoomPlan: the app decides the scan is complete; `RoomCaptureView` then shows a small-scale room for approval. The ML article’s “enough” is indirect: a five-minute scan is presented as long enough to cover rooms up to 15 m × 15 m, and the live notifications exist so the semantic point cloud stays good enough for layout and object detection. Reported detection quality is about those networks (for example 91% precision and 90% recall at 30% 3D IoU across 16 indoor categories), not a user-facing coverage bar. — [RoomCaptureView](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/roomplan/roomcaptureview); [Apple RoomPlan article](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/roomplan)
- Object Capture: `overCapturing` means additional shots will not be used in an on-device reconstruction once the on-device image limit is in effect; otherwise the session stops capturing at the device limit. That is a budget and redundancy stop, not “percent of the wall observed.” Overlap guidance (about 70%, not under 50%) is a capture rule, not an online coverage meter. — [Feedback.overCapturing](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/realitykit/objectcapturesession/feedback); [Capturing photographs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/realitykit/capturing-photographs-for-realitykit-object-capture)
- AR coaching overlay hides when its goal is met (tracking or a requested plane). That goal is not a scanned region. — [ARCoachingOverlayView](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/arcoachingoverlayview)
- ARKit can label mesh faces as floor, wall, ceiling, table, seat, window, or door. That is the semantic vocabulary a “all semantic surfaces observed” rule could use. There is no class for an electrical meter or a gas meter. — [ARMeshClassification](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/armeshclassification)

### Inferences
- A phone app cannot copy Delmerico’s coverage percent, because that percent needs the true surface. The online substitutes that the papers actually use are: information gain or frontier size under a threshold, a hard view budget, or “the app’s author says stop.”
- Diminishing returns in Delmerico (coverage flattens after a handful of good views) supports a practical stop: if qualified new surface inside the region of interest stops appearing, more walking is not helping. That is analogous to their information-gain threshold, computed on mesh area instead of voxel entropy.
- “Floor and wall both have some well-observed faces” is supported by ARKit’s classes. “The meter and the gas meter have been seen” is not; those objects are not in the classification enum, so a semantic-complete stop cannot see them unless the app defines its own region.

### Gaps
- No public RoomPlan or Object Capture document states a numeric coverage threshold.
- No retrieved paper states a standard “uncertainty below X” bound in a form that maps onto ARKit depth confidence. Delmerico’s entropy stop is defined on occupancy probabilities in an octree.

## Where room-scale next-best-view breaks on one outdoor façade

### Takeaway
The classic planners assume a finite object or a finite free-space volume, often with no ground and with views all around the target. A single outdoor wall plus ground is an open half-space: unknown-voxel and frontier scores keep proposing sky, street, and overhead viewpoints a person cannot reach. RoomPlan is built and evaluated for indoor rooms. The fix the surface-frontier paper itself states is a scene boundary that throws away measurements outside it.

### Cited Findings
- Delmerico et al. explicitly consider an object or scene that is unknown but spatially bounded. The test scene is an object in an empty environment with no ground plane, viewed from a closed set of poses around it. — [Delmerico et al. 2017](https://rpg.ifi.uzh.ch/docs/AURO17_Delmerico.pdf)
- SEE’s completion rule “assumes that the observable scene is finite.” The authors’ real-world remedy is “defining a scene boundary and discarding all measurements outside it.” Their large example is still a closed building model (Radcliffe Camera, 40 m) with a bounding box; view distance for the volumetric baselines is half the diagonal of that box (16 m on that model). — [Border, Gammell, and Newman 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08617)
- Langguth and Goesele restrict planning to one or a few objects the camera walks around. On an outdoor statue, the sphere search “generates positions that are way above the ground and cannot be captured casually,” so rejecting views is required. Their volume also grows by 1.5× as points appear, which on an open façade would expand into whatever features happen to match. — [Langguth and Goesele 2013](https://doi.org/10.2312/conf/eg2013/short/093-096)
- Bircher-style and Yamauchi-style scores reward unmapped space. Delmerico notes frontier counting is the exploration formulation, whose goal is unknown environment rather than one object. Outdoors, unmapped space does not end. — [Bircher et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1109/icra.2016.7487281); [Delmerico et al. 2017](https://rpg.ifi.uzh.ch/docs/AURO17_Delmerico.pdf)
- RoomPlan’s published system is interior: walls, doors, windows, floor, ceiling, and indoor object categories (refrigerator, stove, bed, sink, toilet, bath, sofa, washer/dryer, chair, and others). Training data are home interiors scanned with a Faro Focus and iPhone/iPad video, including ceiling and floor. The largest size they design for is about 15 m × 15 m indoors over five minutes. Nothing in the article describes a single exterior façade. — [Apple RoomPlan article](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/roomplan)
- Object Capture is an orbit of one object: move the camera around it, or use a turntable, with large overlap. Feedback includes too close and too far. A wall-mounted meter cannot be orbited, and Apple warns that reflective or low-texture surfaces reconstruct poorly. — [Capturing photographs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/realitykit/capturing-photographs-for-realitykit-object-capture); [Feedback](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/realitykit/objectcapturesession/feedback)
- ARKit mesh classes that exist are ceiling, door, floor, seat, table, wall, window, and none. The reconstructed-scene article notes that mesh ray casts still hit surfaces with little or no visual texture, such as white walls, which is relevant to a painted façade, but the classified object set is indoor. Depth confidence falls under natural light on reflective surfaces and on surfaces that absorb light. — [ARMeshClassification](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/armeshclassification); [reconstructed-scene article](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/visualizing-and-interacting-with-a-reconstructed-scene); [confidence map](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/ardepthdata/confidencemap)
- Massios and Fisher’s point of adding a quality term is that a view which merely sees a surface can still be a bad range image. On a flat wall, a pose looking along the wall is exactly that case: the face is “visible” and the measurement is poor. — [Massios and Fisher 1998](https://doi.org/10.5244/c.12.78)

### Inferences
- An unbounded occupancy frontier in front of a house will prefer the largest unknown region, which is the open yard and sky, not the unscanned corner of the wall. A 3 m × 3 m × 2 m box is the scene boundary SEE says you must add before “no frontiers left” means anything.
- Room-closure rules (a loop of walls, a ceiling, a closed floor plan) do not fire on one façade. Waiting for `ceiling` or for walls to form a room will never finish.
- Near field: Object Capture and RoomPlan only coach “too close / too far” or “move farther away” relative to an object or a detected wall. They do not jointly handle “close enough to read a meter face” and “far enough to cover a 3 m patch of wall.” A graze-angle check matters more here than indoors, because the useful motion is sliding along a plane rather than orbiting a solid.
- Dynamic outdoors (foliage, cars, the person’s own body) will create frontiers the planner should ignore. None of the retrieved NBV papers give an outdoor-façade filter for that; ARKit’s people occlusion is documented only as mesh editing when person segmentation is on, in the scene-reconstruction API text, and was not evaluated here as a guidance signal. — [sceneReconstruction](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/arworldtrackingconfiguration/scenereconstruction)

### Gaps
- A current Apple specification for maximum LiDAR range was not found in the iPhone 12 Pro newsroom text or the ARKit mesh article retrieved on 26 September 2026. Older secondary claims of “5 meters” were not used.
- No retrieved paper measures NBV outdoors on an ARKit mesh of one wall and the ground, or reports how often `wall` / `floor` classification succeeds on an exterior.
- How badly direct sun degrades iPhone LiDAR, beyond Apple’s statement that natural light lowers depth confidence on reflective and absorptive surfaces, was not quantified in these sources.

## A baseline a small team could implement in a few days

### Takeaway
The robust baseline is the cheap, ARKit-native piece of the surface methods: clip the mesh to a fixed box, mark faces that were seen too few times or too edge-on, and point the person at the largest remaining cluster. Stop when that cluster is small or when new well-seen area inside the box stops growing. Do not build a TSDF, do not score 6-DoF information gain, and do not wait for a closed room.

### Cited Findings
- SEE says a real deployment should define a scene boundary and discard measurements outside it, because otherwise the frontier definition assumes a finite scene. — [Border, Gammell, and Newman 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08617)
- Pito’s line of work selects views to cover boundaries of a partial mesh. SEE detects frontiers as the transition from well-supported points to sparse points and aims the next view at that transition. — [Pito 1999](https://doi.org/10.1109/34.799908); [Border, Gammell, and Newman 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08617)
- Massios and Fisher show that visibility alone is the wrong objective if the goal is usable range data; a quality term is required. — [Massios and Fisher 1998](https://doi.org/10.5244/c.12.78)
- Delmerico’s practical stop, when ground truth is unavailable, is “best candidate information gain below a threshold,” and their curves show coverage flattening. Proximity Count, their best simple geometric score, up-weights unobserved cells next to surface that is already seen, which is the same idea as “the hole touching the scanned wall,” without entropy. — [Delmerico et al. 2017](https://rpg.ifi.uzh.ch/docs/AURO17_Delmerico.pdf)
- Inputs that exist on device, with no custom fusion: mesh vertices, faces, and normals; per-face class in {floor, wall, ceiling, table, seat, window, door, none}; camera pose from the AR frame; optional depth confidence `low` / `medium` / `high`. Apple already builds the mesh. The reconstructed-scene sample even walks faces asynchronously so rendering does not stall, which is evidence face iteration is the expected app-side cost. — [ARMeshClassification](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/armeshclassification); [confidence map](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/ardepthdata/confidencemap); [reconstructed-scene article](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/visualizing-and-interacting-with-a-reconstructed-scene)
- Langguth’s user instruction that was actually implemented is an image-plane arrow plus a distance, not a path. RoomPlan’s implemented sentences are short imperatives (“Slow down,” “Move farther away”). Either pattern is a one-line UI. — [Langguth and Goesele 2013](https://doi.org/10.2312/conf/eg2013/short/093-096); [Apple RoomPlan article](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/roomplan)
- What not to port in a few days: Delmerico’s 48-view OctoMap ray caster (research stack, ROS, simulated, bounded object, no phone number); SEE’s full view-adjustment loop (research, aerial-robot plans, simulation); Langguth’s on-device bundle adjustment (2–20 s per new still, dies near 30 images, 2013 phone); RoomPlan’s room-layout network (indoor, unpublished weights); 2026 Gaussian or Bayesian NBV planners. — [Delmerico et al. 2017](https://rpg.ifi.uzh.ch/docs/AURO17_Delmerico.pdf); [Border, Gammell, and Newman 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08617); [Langguth and Goesele 2013](https://doi.org/10.2312/conf/eg2013/short/093-096); [MAGICIAN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22650)

### Inferences
A concrete baseline consistent with those findings:

1. Fix a gravity-aligned box, about 3 m × 3 m × 2 m, on the wall and the ground in front of it. Ignore mesh anchors outside the box. That is SEE’s scene boundary, sized to the equipment rather than to the whole yard.
2. Each frame, take faces inside the box that project into the image. Count a face as a good observation only if the view ray is not too edge-on to the face normal (Massios-style quality) and, if scene depth is available, the corresponding confidence is not `low`. Faces with a low count, and boundary edges that have only one triangle, are the hole set (Pito / SEE frontier, restricted to the box).
3. Cluster those faces. The instruction target is the centroid of the largest cluster. Convert the offset from the camera into a ground-plane direction and one verb: step left or right, move closer, or aim at that patch. If the cluster sits on faces classified `wall` near a depth discontinuity, the verb can say the corner. This is a label for the user, not a scored 6-DoF pose.
4. Optionally also show RoomPlan-style quality lines from quantities ARKit already exposes: large pose delta between frames (“slow down”), low luminance (“more light”), target far from the camera (“move closer”). Those are separate from the hole arrow.
5. Declare enough when the largest hole inside the box is under a size limit, or when the area of faces that have reached the view-count and angle test stops growing for a few seconds. Require some qualifying `wall` faces and some qualifying `floor` faces before saying the structural surfaces are in. Do not require `ceiling`, a closed wall loop, or a class for the meter. The meter and gas meter are covered only to the extent they fall inside the box and produce mesh; ARKit will not name them.

This stays on ARKit mesh anchors, pose, and optional depth confidence. It does not rebuild a TSDF. It will not turn the preview green by itself; it only says whether the box has been seen from enough non-grazing viewpoints. Unmeasured placement rules (footprint clearance, transfer-switch space) stay unknown even after the mesh holes close.

### Gaps
- No retrieved system implements this box-and-view-count guider, so there is no published accuracy or frame-time for it. The claim that it is cheap enough for a hackathon is an inference from “ARKit already built the mesh” plus Langguth’s 900 ms for a much heavier 13³ planner, not a benchmark.
- Thresholds (minimum view count, grazing-angle cutoff, hole size, seconds of diminishing returns) are not given by the papers for this scene. They would be tuned on device.
- Faces that ARKit never instantiates (glass, deep shadow, out of LiDAR confidence) look like holes forever. The baseline cannot tell “not scanned yet” from “this surface will not mesh.” The sources do not provide a test for that distinction beyond the confidence map.
